### The anchoring case: the Post Office Horizon scandal

The capstone case, and the one that answers "why does any of this matter beyond my grade".

Fujitsu's **Horizon** accounting system had, in the words of a Fujitsu executive giving evidence to the public inquiry, "bugs, errors and defects" **from the very start**, known to the parties for nearly two decades. The Post Office prosecuted **hundreds** of sub-postmasters for theft and false accounting on the strength of Horizon data. **Thirteen people took their own lives.** Ninety-three convictions had been overturned before mass exoneration legislation, with roughly 800 more to follow. The inquiry found that senior Post Office and supplier staff **knew or should have known** about the defects, and that evidence of Horizon bugs was **edited out of witness statements**, which the same executive called "shameful and appalling".

Why this is a software engineering ethics case and not only a corporate governance one:

- Someone, repeatedly, made a **technical claim about software they knew to be defective**, in a setting where a person's liberty depended on that claim.
- The defect reports existed. The failure was in what the organization did with them.
- Every engineer in that chain had a moment where saying "the system can produce this discrepancy on its own" was available, and was not taken.

**Teach it as:** the professional obligation attaches to *your statements about your software*, not only to the software. Then land the version that applies to them this semester: when a student says "the agent wrote it", they are making a claim about code they did not read. Same shape, far lower stakes, and the habit forms now or not at all.

Pairs with the graduate-profile reflection in the same week.

**Sources:** Computer Weekly's long-running Horizon coverage is the standing record; the public inquiry's own findings are the authority.
